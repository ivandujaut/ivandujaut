<Abstract label="In short">

On January 1, 2027, Medicare stops requiring its plans to cover Xarelto, the blood thinner Johnson &
Johnson sells in the United States. Eliquis, from Bristol Myers Squibb and Pfizer, stays protected.
[Medicare](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare) is the public insurance for people
over 65 and people with disabilities.

Today Xarelto is covered for 47.85 of the 47.86 million members whose plan and county I could identify,
with no restrictions and on the same copay tier as Eliquis except for 247 people. Coverage is not a commercial win: it is mandatory while the price Medicare negotiated with each manufacturer is in force, although each plan still decides the tier and the restrictions. From 2027 each plan decides, and five companies
hold 74% of the members: they already filed their lists and the lists have not been published.

If volume splits the way members do, and that is an assumption of mine, about 4.8 million prescriptions
a year are at stake, out of the 6.5 million Xarelto had in Medicare in 2025. At the price Medicare negotiated, that whole book bills around $2.29 billion a year, against the
$2.633 billion J&J reports for Xarelto across the United States. That is a ceiling, and even discounted
it leaves most of the brand's American business inside Medicare. Generic versions of the doses that compete with Eliquis have been
approved since 2025 and still record no sale, so each plan's list is what decides.

My recommendation is to fight for that list with the five companies, and to set the floor price for
doing so before sitting down. Below are the numbers, what exactly changes, and what the access team has
in front of it. These are two decisions, in this order: first defend the base, then decide whether to
invest in demand again, which is what I cover in [the other case](/en/projects/xarelto-sin-visita).

</Abstract>

<Thesis>

Xarelto reaches 2027 with no access restrictions and on the same tier as Eliquis, but coverage is mandatory only while the negotiated price is in force, so the one thing it still controls is how much it is
willing to pay for plans to keep covering it the same way.

</Thesis>

## The symptom: an advantage that already flipped

Xarelto came first: the FDA, the agency that approves drugs in the United States, approved it for atrial
fibrillation
[in November 2011](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm?event=overview.process&ApplNo=202439),
and Eliquis
[in December 2012](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm?event=overview.process&ApplNo=202155).
In 2013 it held
[92 of every 100 prescriptions of the two in Medicare](https://data.cms.gov/provider-summary-by-type-of-service/medicare-part-d-prescribers/medicare-part-d-prescribers-by-geography-and-drug).
Eliquis passed it in 2016, by 2024 it was down to 22, and in the first quarter of 2026, according to the
[quarterly spending file](https://data.cms.gov/summary-statistics-on-use-and-payments/medicare-medicaid-spending-by-drug/medicare-quarterly-part-d-spending-by-drug),
18.4.

Sales tell the same story outside Medicare: in 2025 Xarelto billed
[$2.633 billion in the United States](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/200406/000020040626000016/jnj-20251228.htm)
and Eliquis,
[$10.239 billion](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/14272/000001427226000004/bmy-20251231.htm).
It lost that lead with no access restrictions and while spending more than its rival on physicians,
which I cover in [the other case](/en/projects/xarelto-sin-visita). What follows is what it still has to
defend.

## The restriction that was not there

[Every plan publishes its formulary](https://data.cms.gov/dataset/monthly-prescription-drug-plan-formulary-and-pharmacy-network-information),
the list of drugs it covers and under what conditions, such as step therapy (try another drug first) or
the copay tier (how much the patient pays).

On Xarelto's 10, 15 and 20 mg tablets, the ones that compete with Eliquis, **no Medicare plan required
step therapy in 2023 or in 2026**, nor prior authorization, and in 2026 they sit on the same tier as
Eliquis
[for every member with an identified plan and county except 247](https://www.cms.gov/data-research/statistics-trends-and-reports/medicare-advantagepart-d-contract-and-enrollment-data).
In 2023 the only restriction on those tablets was on Eliquis, in one UnitedHealth formulary (839,524
members). Step therapy does exist on two presentations that, according to Xarelto's
[label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=10db92f9-2300-4a80-836b-673e1ae91610), are
used for other cases: the oral suspension, approved for children (9.47 million members, all at Humana),
and the 2.5 mg tablet, for coronary or peripheral artery disease (448,621).

In commercial insurance, access also worked in its favor: the only exclusion by a large pharmacy benefit
manager (PBM) was against Eliquis. The
[one run by the largest pharmacy chain dropped it from its formulary in 2022 "in favor of Janssen's Xarelto"](https://www.drugchannels.net/2022/01/five-takeaways-from-big-three-pbms-2022.html)
and [put it back in July](https://www.managedhealthcareexecutive.com/view/cvs-caremark-puts-eliquis-back-on-its-formularies).
Express Scripts
[excluded neither of them between 2019 and 2026](https://www.express-scripts.com/art/open_enrollment/DrugListExclusionsAndAlternatives.pdf).
J&J tells physicians today that Xarelto has
[coverage for more than 99% of commercial and Part D patients](https://www.xareltohcp.com/access/).

## The deal with Medicare, and what changes in 2027

Since 2026, Xarelto and Eliquis have a
[negotiated price with Medicare](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/fact-sheet-negotiated-prices-initial-price-applicability-year-2026.pdf),
inside the program created by the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA): $197 for 30 days of Xarelto, 62% below
its 2023 list price, and $231 for Eliquis. The deal has three parts:

- **Every plan has to cover it** while the price is in force.
- **The manufacturer does not pay the Part D discounts**; instead,
  [the government pays the plan a subsidy of 10% of the negotiated price](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/final-cy-2026-part-d-redesign-program-instructions).
- **It is not entirely voluntary:** according to
  [CMS guidance](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/medicare-drug-price-negotiation-final-guidance-ipay-2027-and-manufacturer-effectuation-mfp-2026-2027.pdf),
  the agency that runs Medicare, a manufacturer only leaves without penalties if it also exits the Part D discount program and
  Medicaid. The Supreme Court declined to review the cases of
  [Janssen](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-749.html)
  and
  [Bristol Myers Squibb](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-751.html)
  in May 2026.

The effect shows up in spending: in the first quarter of 2026, Medicare paid
[$374 per Xarelto prescription](https://data.cms.gov/summary-statistics-on-use-and-payments/medicare-medicaid-spending-by-drug/medicare-quarterly-part-d-spending-by-drug),
against $1,006 in 2025.

CMS removed Xarelto from the program
[once generic competition arrived](https://ncpa.org/newsroom/qam/2025/12/02/entresto-stelara-and-xarelto-will-exit-medicare-drug-price-negotiation),
so on January 1, 2027 it loses mandatory coverage and goes back to paying the Part D discounts. From there each plan also decides whether to cover it, on top of the tier and the restrictions it already decided, with the only general rule being to cover
[at least two drugs in each class](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/prescription-drug-coverage/prescriptiondrugcovcontra/downloads/part-d-benefits-manual-chapter-6.pdf).
Eliquis stays in the program in 2027, at
[$237.25](https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/medicare-prescription-drug-affordability/overview/medicare-drug-price-negotiation-program/selected-drugs-negotiated-prices),
and cannot have a generic
[before April 1, 2028](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/78003/000007800326000026/pfe-20251231.htm).
That date sets the window: 19 months remain until both products can go generic, and whatever is invested
now has to pay for itself inside that period. The date is subject to appeals.

**The generics: approved, with no visible sale.** Pharmacies buy the 2.5 mg one at $0.66 per tablet, against $9.78 for the
brand, according to the
[pharmacy acquisition price survey](https://data.medicaid.gov/dataset/fbb83258-11c7-47f5-8b18-5f8e79f7e704).
The 10, 15 and 20 mg ones, which compete with Eliquis,
[have 16 approved manufacturers](https://www.fda.gov/media/76860/download) since May 2025, but in 2026 no
purchase shows up in that survey and they do not appear in
[Medicare formularies](https://data.cms.gov/dataset/monthly-prescription-drug-plan-formulary-and-pharmacy-network-information).
The only patent still listed on those strengths is the once-daily one, through February 2034, with
pediatric exclusivity to August, and the
suits against the manufacturers ended in dismissals and in at least one
[confidential settlement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/200406/000020040626000087/jnj-20260329.htm),
in January 2026. That those settlements set an entry date is my inference.

Outside the United States, where generics already arrived, Bayer's Xarelto sales
[fell 31.6% in 2025](https://reports.bayer.com/annual-report-2025/en/report-on-economic-position/earnings-asset-and-financial-position/business-development-by-division/pharmaceuticals.html)
and
[41.4% in the first half of 2026](https://reports.bayer.com/half-year-financial-report-q2-2026/en/interim-group-management-report/sales-earnings-and-financial-position/business-development-by-division/pharmaceuticals.html),
currency adjusted.

## The decision in front of it

**Whose decision it is, and what the goal is.** It belongs to J&J's market access team in the United
States, which answers for those $2.29 billion: reach 2027 with the coverage and the tier Xarelto has
today, and find out in time if any of the large plans moves.

**Where it can go, and why it can.** Access is the one thing it still controls without a sales force.
Five companies hold 74% of the members, according to
[KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicare/medicare-part-d-enrollment-premiums-and-cost-sharing-in-2026/): five
tables, not hundreds. And it has something to bring to the table: leaving the program restores its
freedom to set its price, while Eliquis cannot charge more than $237.25 per 30 days through 2027. Today Medicare pays $374 per
Xarelto prescription and $399 for an Eliquis one, but the prescriptions do not carry the same number of
days: normalized to 30-day fills with the 2024 fills-per-prescription ratio, they are about $210 against $248, so per month of therapy Medicare
already pays less for Xarelto.

There is also a number it already knows: the price it accepted for 2026, $197 per 30 days, sits $40
below the one protecting Eliquis in 2027. Whatever Eliquis offers below that ceiling, in rebates, is not public, and neither are the plan's margins.

**What is at stake, in dollars.** Xarelto had
[6,511,197 prescriptions in Medicare in 2025](https://data.cms.gov/summary-statistics-on-use-and-payments/medicare-medicaid-spending-by-drug/medicare-quarterly-part-d-spending-by-drug).
That is about 11.6 million 30-day fills, converted with the fills-per-prescription ratio I measured in
2024, and at the negotiated price they bill $2.29 billion a year, 87% of what
J&J reports for Xarelto across the United States. The same arithmetic for Eliquis gives $9.811 billion
against $10.239 billion reported, and Eliquis also sells outside Medicare: the negotiated price landed
above the average net price, so my number is a ceiling for both. Even after that discount, most of
the business runs through Medicare. If volume splits the way members do, and that is an assumption of
mine, about 4.8 million prescriptions a year depend on those five decisions, and each share point of the
pair is worth 328,702 prescriptions, around $115.6 million.

**The first step: the 2027 map, and why that one.** The 2027 lists were filed
[between May 11 and June 1, 2026](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/cy-2027-part-d-bidding-instructions-final-g.pdf):
the decision is already made and still cannot be seen. As soon as CMS publishes the
[2027 formulary](https://data.cms.gov/dataset/monthly-prescription-drug-plan-formulary-and-pharmacy-network-information),
the job is to sort plan by plan into four buckets: keeps the tier, lowers it, adds a restriction, or
drops it. It comes out of the same file this analysis uses and says which table to sit at. For 2027 what
remains are the change windows CMS opens during the year; the round that can be won whole closes the
first Monday of June 2027.

**The two branches, and how to tell which one it is in.** With no 10, 15 or 20 mg generic at the
counter, each plan's list decides those $2.29 billion and the fight is worth what it costs. With a
generic, the pharmacy substitutes on its own, the brand's tier stops mattering, and the play becomes
harvesting or selling its own generic. Both warnings are free and monthly: purchases in the price survey
and appearances in the formularies.

**The definitions, before measuring.** For this to repeat every month and be comparable against last
year, three rules have to be written down: that coverage is counted by presentation and not by molecule,
that the tier is always compared against Eliquis, and that each plan is weighted by the members in that
same month's enrollment file. Without that, two reports on the same file give different numbers.

**What I would measure, and how far.**

_2027 coverage: do plans hold it without the mandate?_

- **Primary:** members with Xarelto's tablets covered, today 47.85 of 47.86 million.
- **Guardrail:** those on the same tier as Eliquis (today all but 247) and those with a quantity limit,
  today even between the two (43.7 million against 43.6).
- **Early warning:** any of the five large companies adding step therapy or prior authorization, today
  zero on both products.
- **Line in the sand:** above 95% covered and at least 80% on the same tier, there is no need to give up
  price; below that, it is negotiated plan by plan; if one asks for a net price that breaks the margin
  floor, it is let go.

_Volume, which warns earlier than the formulary_

- **Primary:** the quarter's Xarelto prescriptions, 1,465,876 in the first quarter of 2026, which the
  spending file publishes months before the formulary.
- **Line in the sand:** two straight quarters below the trend of the previous four force a return to the
  negotiating table without waiting for the list.

**What each thing costs.** With 6.5 million prescriptions, every dollar per prescription given up is
$6.5 million, and it has to be recovered inside the 19 months left before the Eliquis generic. Payback
(how long it takes to earn back what was invested) also needs one internal number: the margin on a
prescription.

**What I would not build.**

- **Parity at any price.** Buying volume below the margin floor trades money for prescriptions that do
  not pay.
- **An access campaign aimed at physicians.** On the competing tablets there is no restriction to fight
  against.
- **A copay program for Medicare.** The law does not allow it: manufacturer savings cards exclude public
  plans.

**The kill criterion and the trade-off (what is being risked).** If in the first quarter of 2027 coverage
stays above 95% and parity above 80% without giving up price, there is nothing to spend. If holding them
costs more than the margin they defend, the right call is to let those plans go and protect the margin,
which is the strategy J&J has already been applying with physician detailing.

## What this analysis cannot support

- **Net prices with the plans.** They are confidential; the comparison here uses the negotiated price and
  the spending per prescription Medicare publishes, and the $2.29 billion is not net sales.
- **That a 10, 15 or 20 mg generic reaches the counter.** They are approved and record no purchases.
- **Nothing clinical, and nothing about the commercial market.** It does not say one blood thinner is
  better than another, and the prescriptions are Medicare only.

## What would change my mind

What would refute the thesis:

- **The 2027 formulary showing the same coverage and the same tier without J&J giving up price.** There
  would be nothing to negotiate.
- **A 10, 15 or 20 mg generic going on sale.** The problem would stop being the plan's list and become
  the price at the counter.
- **Large plans moving Eliquis instead of Xarelto.** The pressure would fall on the other side.

This is independent work, built from public sources. I have no commercial relationship, past or present,
with Johnson & Johnson, Bayer, Bristol Myers Squibb or Pfizer.
