<Abstract label="TL;DR">

In Argentine insurance, the bad ending of the collections story is not always "the customer
refused to pay". Part of the arrears is manufactured by the process: the debit bounced and nobody
retried in time, the broker collected but didn't remit, the employer misdeclared payroll, the
receipt never reconciled and the policy got cancelled anyway. I mapped the market's four collection flows and
quantified them with four official sources: the workers' comp system fails to collect **$45.3
billion pesos every month** (8.36% arrears rate, SRT), **for every 100 property & casualty
policies issued, 19 are annulled** (SSN), the median top-50 insurer carries **the
equivalent of 2 months of premium locked in receivables**, and annulments eat **10% of issued
premium in pesos** (SSN financial statements). The pattern that ties it together: friction grows
with intermediation and with how small the payer is. The thesis of this case is that
process-manufactured arrears are the cheapest to recover and the most expensive to ignore, and
that merely accelerating reconciliation frees months of premium trapped on the balance sheet. I
propose where to start, what to build and which metrics should govern it.

</Abstract>

## How money enters an insurer (four flows, four ways to break)

Insurance collections is not one process: it's at least four, with different actors and different
failure modes. I surveyed and diagrammed them before touching a single dataset, because a number
without its flow explains nothing.

The original diagram, with the working notes from the research, is
[navigable in Eraser](https://app.eraser.io/workspace/Q3fl0Fp1sk8eu23z3301).

1. **Automatic debit (personal lines).** The debit attempt bounces (insufficient funds, expired
   card), there are 2-3 blind retries, and if they fail: arrears → notice → suspension →
   cancellation. Customers often find out when they no longer have coverage.
2. **Broker-mediated collections (PAS).** The broker collects from the customer in cash, check or
   transfer, and remits to the insurer whenever they remit. Reconciling what was collected against
   the core system is manual, with human error and structural delay.
3. **Workers' comp (ART).** The employer self-declares payroll through [form F.931](https://www.afip.gob.ar/declaracionenlinea/),
   the monthly sworn payroll declaration filed with Argentina's tax agency (AFIP/ARCA) that
   determines social-security charges, including the ART premium. The premium varies month to
   month with that declared payroll, and reconciling declared vs. collected drags
   under-declaration, error and arrears.
4. **Regulated multi-channel (Mexico, as contrast).** Direct debit, bank counter, OXXO, SPEI, card:
   every channel emits a different receipt, all reconciled by hand against the core. If
   reconciliation doesn't land in time, the policy is cancelled _improperly_: the customer had
   already paid.

The common denominator is not non-payment: it's that **between the money and the system that
decides whether a policy lives, there is a manual process**. That is what this case sets out to
measure.

## The numbers: four official sources, one problem

### 45 billion pesos a month: workers' comp arrears as measured by the regulator itself

The SRT [publishes monthly](https://www.srt.gob.ar/estadisticas/cf_ultimos_datos.php) how much
every employer in the system should have paid (agreed premium) and how much actually came in
(collected premium). In April 2026: $542.2 billion pesos agreed, $496.9 billion collected. The
gap, **$45.3 billion in a single month**, is exactly the "Morosidad: 8.36%" the agency itself
reports. Annualized, the order of magnitude is ~8.4% of a ~$6.5 trillion peso revenue base.

The segment split is consistent with the thesis, though there is an alternative reading these
figures cannot rule out: smaller payers, more volatile income. The system splits into two
universes: **productive units** (companies and businesses) and **private households** (families
employing domestic staff).

| Group              | Monthly arrears | % of agreed |
| ------------------ | --------------- | ----------- |
| Productive units   | $44.4B          | 8.29%       |
| Private households | $0.9B           | **14.25%**  |
| **Whole system**   | **$45.3B**      | **8.36%**   |

98% of those arrears are companies. Productive units cover 9.5M workers: they agreed $535.9B, paid
$491.5B and left $44.4B uncollected. Private households cover 601k: they agreed $6.3B, paid $5.4B
and left $0.9B.

The volume sits on one side and the signal on the other. Private households are 6% of the workers
and 2% of the arrears, but they fail at nearly twice the rate: 14.25% against 8.29%. That is the
segment with the smallest payer and the most manual process, which is exactly what the thesis
predicts. Per covered worker, the system agrees $58,730 pesos a month, collects $54,463 and leaves
$4,266 behind.

### For every 100 policies issued in a quarter, 19 are annulled

The property & casualty market annuls **3.14 million policies per quarter** against 16.6 million
gross issuances (net issuance plus annulments). That is 19 out of every 100. It comes from the SSN's
[policies, claims and premiums release](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/superintendencia-de-seguros/estadisticas/polizas-siniestros-y-primas)
for Q1 2026.

The pattern by line of business repeats the SRT's. Motorcycles, the most precarious segment, annul
25.6% of what they issue and 18% of their in-force stock _per quarter_. Auto, the mass-market line,
annuls 1.38 million policies per quarter.

### The cost lives on the balance sheet: 2 months of premium trapped

The median top-50 insurer carries **the equivalent of 2 months of invoiced premium not yet
collected**, but the number depends on the sales channel more than on the company: it ranges from
0.3 to 4.6 months depending on how the money is collected. On the balance sheet, premium receivables
are money already invoiced that hasn't come in yet; divided by an average month of premium, they
show how many months of sales are pending, which is any industry's DSO (days sales outstanding),
here expressed in months.

The data comes from the
[quarterly financial statements](https://datosabiertos.ssn.gob.ar/dataset/balances) insurers file
with the SSN (March 2026 close, 177 companies). The same source shows **annulments in pesos eating
10% of premium**, with extremes: Seguros Galicia 24.6%, Nación Seguros 24.2%.

At the top of the ranking sits everything intermediated: Assurant (sells warranties through
retailers), the cooperatives with broker networks (La Segunda, Cooperación Mutual, Río Uruguay) and
the bank and institutional channel (Allianz, Galicia, Nación, Provincia Seguros). At the bottom,
direct collection: La Caja with automatic debit (0.9 months) and Provincia ART with payroll-based
collection (0.3). With an intermediary in the money flow, premium takes 3-4 months to arrive;
without one, weeks. It's flow 2 of the diagram, confirmed company by company in the accounting.

One precision: part of those months is commercial design (installment plans, remittance cycles
agreed with the broker), not pathology. The channel gradient tells you where to look, not how much
to recover.

The year-over-year comparison (same fiscal month, 2025 vs 2026) is flat: median 2.06 → 1.98 months.
**The problem is structural, not cyclical.**

## The thesis

<Thesis>
  Insurers do have a collections problem, but not all arrears are equal. Part of them is
  manufactured by the process itself: payments that existed and never reconciled, debits that
  bounced without a smart retry, cancelled customers who wanted to pay. That part is the cheapest to
  recover, the most expensive to ignore, and the only one product can fix.
</Thesis>

Part, not all: with public data you cannot measure what share of the arrears is genuine
(customers who can't or won't pay) and what share the process produces. Every number in this case
mixes both. The SRT gap includes real insolvency on top of under-declaration and error; the 18.9%
annulment rate includes voluntary churn and technical causes; the months of premium in receivables
include commercial credit by design, not just pathology. Claiming "the problem is reconciliation"
would be a statement these figures cannot prove.

What the figures do show is where the recoverable value per peso invested is concentrated, for
three reasons:

1. **Asymmetric error costs.** Losing a genuine defaulter costs about one month of premium,
   because coverage stops. Improperly cancelling a paying customer costs their full lifetime
   value plus the acquisition cost of replacing them. Even if manufactured arrears are a minority
   of the stock, their unit cost is a multiple.
2. **The working capital does not depend on the decomposition.** The top 50 carries **$4.13
   trillion pesos in premium receivables** against a monthly premium of $2.13 trillion (SSN
   financial statements, March 2026). At 2-4% monthly rates, holding that stock costs **$1 to $2
   trillion pesos per year**; accelerating collection by one month frees ~$2.1 trillion once.
   This value exists even if every peso of arrears were genuine: it's money that was arriving
   anyway, arriving earlier.
3. **Tractability.** A customer's insolvency cannot be fixed with software; retry timing, receipt
   matching and self-service payment updates can. As a reference for the recoverable order of
   magnitude, in digital subscriptions [20% to 40% of churn is involuntary](https://gocardless.com/es/guias/consejos/churn-involuntario),
   a benchmark from another industry, useful as an order of magnitude rather than sector data.

## Prioritization: where I would start

Four flows, one team: you have to choose. My criterion is volume × automatability × the channel's
political friction.

1. **Automatic debit, first.** Maximum volume (the auto line alone issues 7 million net policies
   per quarter), zero humans with their own incentives in the middle, and the intervention is pure
   product: retries with smart timing, multi-channel dunning, self-service payment-method updates.
   It's the flow where software alone can move the needle.
2. **Brokers, second, and as a product for the broker, not against them.** It's the most expensive
   flow (3-4 months of premium trapped at intermediated carriers) and the hardest: the broker is a
   channel with interests, not a process. The play is not to disintermediate but to give them
   automatic remittance: make remitting stop being work.
3. **Workers' comp, third.** The arrears are huge in pesos but the flow depends on employer
   self-declaration and the tax agency; the lever is early detection of under-declaration, not
   classic dunning.

## What I would build: the collections agent

Three concrete design decisions, each with its why:

- **Retries that know when there's money.** The blind 48-hour retry is the policy of someone who
  doesn't look at data. The right timing can be learned: salary-deposit dates, the customer's own
  bounce history, day of the month. Goal: maximize first-retry approval, not retry count.
- **Dunning that offers a way out, not a threat.** Between the bounce and the suspension there's a
  window that today is used to notify and should be used to resolve: a WhatsApp message with a
  payment link and self-service payment-method update. Cancellation becomes the end of a path with
  exits, not a timeout.
- **Reconciliation where humans only review exceptions.** Automatic matching of receipts and
  remittances against the core; people stop hunting for matches and start resolving what the
  system flags. Improper cancellation (cancelling a paid policy) becomes an auditable event, not a
  statistical accident.

## How I would govern it

The North Star: **% of issued premium collected on time without human intervention.** It measures
value delivered (the money that comes in _and_ the manual army no longer needed), it's a leading
indicator of the financial result, and every flow feeds it with inputs the team can move:

| Flow    | Input metrics                                                                    |
| ------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Debit   | First-attempt approval · post-bounce recovery · days bounce→regularization       |
| Brokers | Days collection→remittance · % auto-reconciled remittances · aging of open items |
| ART     | Agreed−collected gap (the SRT figure) · days to detect under-declaration         |

With two guardrails so the metric can't be optimized by breaking something else: complaints about
improper charges (the goal is collecting smarter, not harder) and cost per peso collected. Plus a
warning about the metric usually crowned in collections: recovery rate **rewards the flow failing**
so it can be recovered. Recovery is an input; the star is the payment going through the first time.

<Callout type="warning" title="Assumptions and limits">
  This is an outside-in analysis built on public data, and it carries the limits of that starting
  point:

1. The SSN's "annulment" includes non-financial causes (endorsements, policy rewrites when a
   vehicle changes), so 18.9% is a ceiling, not a pure measure of collections churn. It also
   compares flows of the same period rather than a cohort: a policy annulled in the quarter may
   have been issued earlier.

2. The SRT's monthly arrears is not a definitive loss: part is collected later with interest; the
   correct reading is "money that didn't arrive on time".

3. The involuntary-churn benchmark (20-40%) comes from digital subscriptions, carried over as an
   order of magnitude.

4. Galeno ART did not report in the Q1 2026 financial-statements file: it's missing from the
   ranking for lack of data, not by omission.

5. The statements dataset mixes number formats across periods; I used the two periods with intact
   formatting (March 2025 and March 2026 closes) and discarded the corrupted ones.

6. Arrears rates per segment come from the unrounded amounts. Redoing the maths with the rounded
   figures in the table gives 14.29% for private households, against the 14.25% published.

</Callout>
