<Abstract label="TL;DR">

In Argentine insurance, the bad ending of the collections story is almost never "the customer
refused to pay". It's arrears manufactured by the process: the debit bounced and nobody retried in
time, the broker collected but didn't remit, the employer misdeclared payroll, the receipt never
reconciled and the policy got cancelled anyway. I mapped the market's four collection flows and
quantified them with four official sources: the workers' comp system fails to collect **$45.3
billion pesos every month** (8.36% arrears rate, SRT), **18.9% of property & casualty policies
issued are annulled within the same quarter** (SSN), the median top-50 insurer carries **the
equivalent of 2 months of premium locked in receivables**, and annulments eat **10% of issued
premium in pesos** (SSN financial statements). The pattern that ties it together: friction grows
with intermediation and with how small the payer is. The thesis of this case is that you don't fix
this by collecting more aggressively but by reconciling better — and I propose where to start, what
to build and which metrics should govern it.

</Abstract>

## How money enters an insurer (four flows, four ways to break)

Insurance collections is not one process: it's at least four, with different actors and different
failure modes. I surveyed and diagrammed them before touching a single dataset, because a number
without its flow explains nothing.

All four converge into a collections agent that reconciles against the insurer's core system. Diagram labels are in Spanish."
  width={920}

1. **Automatic debit (personal lines).** The debit attempt bounces (insufficient funds, expired
   card), there are 2-3 blind retries, and if they fail: arrears → notice → suspension →
   cancellation. Customers often find out when they no longer have coverage.
2. **Broker-mediated collections (PAS).** The broker collects from the customer in cash, check or
   transfer, and remits to the insurer whenever they remit. Reconciling what was collected against
   the core system is manual, with human error and structural delay.
3. **Workers' comp (ART).** The employer self-declares payroll (form F.931), the premium varies
   month to month with the declared headcount, and reconciling declared vs. collected drags
   under-declaration, error and arrears.
4. **Regulated multi-channel (Mexico, as contrast).** Direct debit, bank counter, OXXO, SPEI, card:
   every channel emits a different receipt, all reconciled by hand against the core. If
   reconciliation doesn't land in time, the policy is cancelled _improperly_ — the customer had
   paid.

The common denominator is not non-payment: it's that **between the money and the system that
decides whether a policy lives, there is a manual process**. That is what this case sets out to
measure.

## The numbers: four official sources, one problem

### Workers' comp arrears is the regulator's own metric, not an opinion

The SRT publishes agreed and collected premium for the system monthly. In April 2026: $542.2
billion pesos agreed, $496.9 billion collected. The gap — **$45.3 billion in a single month** — is
exactly the "Morosidad: 8.36%" the agency itself reports. Annualized, the order of magnitude is
~8.4% of a ~$6.5 trillion peso revenue base.

The segment split is the first piece of evidence for the thesis:

| Segment                             | Arrears    | Monthly gap |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| Productive units (companies)        | 8.29%      | $44.4B      |
| Private households (domestic staff) | **14.25%** | $0.9B       |

Where the payer is smaller and the process more manual, arrears nearly double. In money per worker:
$58,730 pesos are agreed per month and $54,463 collected — $4,266 stuck in limbo.

### Almost 1 in 5 policies issued is annulled within the same quarter

From the SSN's policies, claims and premiums release (Q1 2026): of 16.6 million gross property &
casualty policies issued in the quarter, **3.14 million were annulled**. And the pattern by line of
business repeats the SRT's: motorcycles (the most precarious segment) annul 25.6% of what they
issue and 18% of their in-force stock _per quarter_; auto, the mass-market line, annuls 1.38
million policies per quarter.

### The cost lives on the balance sheet: 2 months of premium trapped

From the quarterly financial statements insurers file with the SSN (March 2026 close, 177
companies): the median top-50 insurer by premium carries **the equivalent of 2 months of issued
premium locked in premium receivables**, and **annulments in pesos eat 10% of premium** (with
extremes: Seguros Galicia 24.6%, Nación Seguros 24.2%).

But the most useful finding in the ranking is _who_ sits at the top and who at the bottom:

**Months trapped are a function of the channel.** At the top of the ranking: Assurant (sells
warranties through retailers), the cooperatives with broker networks (La Segunda, Cooperación
Mutual, Río Uruguay) and the bank/institutional channel (Allianz, Galicia, Nación, Provincia
Seguros). At the bottom, with healthy collections: La Caja (direct sales, automatic debit: 0.9
months) and Provincia ART (payroll-based collection: 0.3). Wherever an intermediary sits in the
money flow, premium takes 3-4 months to arrive; where collection is direct, weeks. It's flow 2 of
the diagram, confirmed company by company in the accounting.

The year-over-year comparison (same fiscal month, 2025 vs 2026) is flat: median 2.06 → 1.98 months.
**The problem is structural, not cyclical.**

## The thesis

> Insurers don't have a collections problem: they have a reconciliation problem. A relevant
> fraction of the arrears they see is false arrears — customers who paid or wanted to pay, trapped
> in a process that doesn't retry in time, doesn't match receipts, and can't tell "won't pay" from
> "couldn't pay".

In digital subscriptions, 20% to 40% of churn is involuntary — recoverable with smart retries and
dunning. Argentine insurance operates with more intermediation and more payment-method friction
than a SaaS, not less. Every point of that arrears that is involuntary is revenue lost without
anyone having decided it: not the customer, who wanted coverage, nor the insurer, who already paid
to acquire them.

## Prioritization: where I would start

Four flows, one team: you have to choose. My criterion is volume × automatability × the channel's
political friction.

1. **Automatic debit, first.** Maximum volume (the auto line alone issues 7 million net policies
   per quarter), zero humans with their own incentives in the middle, and the intervention is pure
   product: retries with smart timing, multi-channel dunning, self-service payment-method updates.
   It's the flow where software alone can move the needle.
2. **Brokers, second — but as a product for the broker, not against them.** It's the most expensive
   flow (3-4 months of premium trapped at intermediated carriers) and the hardest: the broker is a
   channel with interests, not a process. The play is not to disintermediate but to give them
   automatic remittance — make remitting stop being work.
3. **Workers' comp, third.** The arrears are huge in pesos but the flow depends on employer
   self-declaration and the tax agency; the lever is early detection of under-declaration, not
   classic dunning.

## What I would build: the collections agent

Three concrete design decisions, each with its why:

- **Retries that know when there's money.** The blind 48-hour retry is the policy of someone who
  doesn't look at data. The right timing can be learned: salary-deposit dates, the customer's own
  bounce history, day of the month. Goal: maximize first-retry approval, not retry count.
- **Dunning that offers a way out, not a threat.** Between the bounce and the suspension there's a
  window that today is used to notify and should be used to resolve: a WhatsApp message with a
  payment link and self-service payment-method update. Cancellation becomes the end of a path with
  exits, not a timeout.
- **Reconciliation where humans only review exceptions.** Automatic matching of receipts and
  remittances against the core; people stop hunting for matches and start resolving what the
  system flags. Improper cancellation (cancelling a paid policy) becomes an auditable event, not a
  statistical accident.

## How I would govern it

The North Star: **% of issued premium collected on time without human intervention.** It measures
value delivered (the money that comes in _and_ the manual army no longer needed), it's a leading
indicator of the financial result, and every flow feeds it with inputs the team can move:

| Flow    | Input metrics                                                                    |
| ------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Debit   | First-attempt approval · post-bounce recovery · days bounce→regularization       |
| Brokers | Days collection→remittance · % auto-reconciled remittances · aging of open items |
| ART     | Agreed−collected gap (the SRT figure) · days to detect under-declaration         |

With two guardrails so the metric can't be optimized by breaking something else: complaints about
improper charges (the goal is collecting smarter, not harder) and cost per peso collected. Plus a
warning about the metric usually crowned in collections: recovery rate **rewards the flow failing**
so it can be recovered. Recovery is an input; the star is the payment going through the first time.

<Callout type="warning" title="Assumptions and limits, stated">
  This is an outside-in analysis built on public data; what I couldn't observe, I say so. (1) The
  SSN's "annulment" includes non-financial causes — endorsements, policy rewrites when a vehicle
  changes — so 18.9% is a ceiling, not a pure measure of collections churn. (2) The SRT's monthly
  arrears is not a definitive loss: part is collected later with interest; the correct reading is
  "money that didn't arrive on time". (3) The involuntary-churn benchmark (20-40%) comes from
  digital subscriptions, carried over as an order of magnitude. (4) Galeno ART did not report in the
  Q1 2026 financial-statements file: it's missing from the ranking for lack of data, not by
  omission. (5) The statements dataset mixes number formats across periods; I used the two periods
  with intact formatting (March 2025 and March 2026 closes) and discarded the corrupted ones.
</Callout>
